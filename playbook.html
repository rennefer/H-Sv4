Horus and Seth

Hieratic word list from the Papyrus Chester Beatty

A project by Katrine Iversen

How we taught the site to find every word of Wenamun and the Two Brothers on the hieratic photographs — the steps we took, the problems we ran into, and how we worked through them together, one line and one word at a time.

Manuscripts Wenamun 93 lines · Two Brothers 184 lines Words boxed 5,611 Checked by hand so far 2,032 Written by Claude, with Katrine Iversen

01 Where we started

We had two manuscripts on the site, the Report of Wenamun and the Tale of Two Brothers. For each line we had a photograph of the hieratic, the scribe's own handwriting, and a hieroglyphic transcription of the same line: Erin Tarasi's for Wenamun, and Moldenke's 1898 edition for the Two Brothers. We also had the dictionary built from Horus and Seth, nearly five thousand photographed words written in a very similar hand.

What we did not have was the link between the two pictures. A reader could see that a line said pA “the” somewhere, but not where on the papyrus the scribe had written it. Our goal was simple to say and long to do: a box around every word on every hieratic photo, so that hovering a word in the transcription lights up the same word in the scribe's hand, and so every one of those words can be collected, compared and, in the end, added to the dictionary as a clean new example.

02 First, reading the transcription

Before we could look for a word on the papyrus, we needed to know exactly where each word sits in the transcription. We split every transcription line into numbered sign groups and gave each line its own word list (for the Two Brothers, Moldenke's glossary entries for that line). Helper agents then read each line image and recorded which numbered groups make up which word.

A small checking script caught the mechanical mistakes — a group used twice, a word left without signs — and drew every result as a review picture that had to be looked at before a line counted as done. Two kinds of words needed their own rule from the start: small words written above or below each other in one group (marked as “stacked”), and side-by-side words fused into one group. That gave us the transcription boxes you still see on the manuscript pages today.

03 One good line, then one bad one

Katrine then asked the real question: could we find the same words on the hieratic photo, using the transcription as a map? We did not start with the whole papyrus. We tried one clear Wenamun line (2.10) and looked at the result together. It was good, so Katrine asked for a bad one: line 1.11, broken at the right and stained through the middle.

That second test showed both what makes the method work and where it stops. In 1.11 we had found the number 11 at the end of the line, which has no photo in the dictionary, and Katrine asked how. Numerals have shapes you can know without a reference (an upside-down V for ten, an upright stroke for one), and the number had to be the last word of the line. The other words in that line were much harder. Several sit in the break and could only be placed by their order. So every box carries an honest flag: sure when the shape itself is recognised, unsure when it is placed by order, sits in a break, or its border with the neighbouring word is a guess.

The method we settled on, for every line

  1. Prepare the line

    One folder per line: the word list in reading order, the transcription with every word boxed and numbered, dictionary photos of the words that have one, and the hieratic photo enlarged in three overlapping pieces with a ruler in percent of the photo width.

  2. Anchors first

    Find the words that can be recognised with certainty: from a dictionary photo, from well-known sign shapes (numerals, tall reed strokes, the long horizontal n, seated-man determinatives, plural strokes), or because the same word occurs twice in the line.

  3. Fill in between

    The text runs right to left and the order never changes, so the words between two anchors share out the ink that is left. The spacing is not proportional to the transcription; hieratic groups are wider or narrower than the printed signs.

  4. Write the boxes

    For each word: left and right edge in percent, sure or unsure, and when needed “top”/“bot” for stacked words, “all” for a fused group, or an exact height for a small sign that only fills part of the line.

  5. Check, look, correct

    A checker refuses missing words, overlaps and words out of order, then draws the boxes on the photo above the transcription. No line was finished without someone looking at that picture and fixing boxes that cut through a sign, took a neighbour's ink or sat on bare papyrus.

04 How we read a word on the line

Finding a word on the papyrus is reading, not measuring. We did not start out knowing that. Before Wenamun and the Two Brothers we spent a long time trying to find words on the Horus and Seth lines by computer matching. We slid known pictures of a word along the line and scored how alike the ink was. Each failure there taught us something we now do on purpose.

Scores do not read
Two genuine examples of the same word in this hand often scored no more than 0.35 to 0.40 alike, while a dense blob of unrelated ink could score higher. A number can tell you where to look. It cannot tell you what is written there.
Words do not stop where the ink stops
Cutting a line at the gaps between ink fails on cursive hieratic. Words run into each other with no pen-lift, and one word can leave a gap in its own middle.
One picture per word is not enough
The same word can be written in very different shapes. Hr as a preposition and Hr as the god's name hardly look alike, so each spelling needs its own examples.
Check the reference before trusting it
Some reference pictures were filed under the wrong word, and a comparison sheet that cut images to one width showed only half a sign. Both fooled us until we looked at full, checked examples.
Look for the anchor shape
What worked was naming the one feature a word cannot do without, then finding that feature in the line. For Hr it is the thick full-height stroke, for st the closed loop over a small hook, for Dd the hooked knob running into a fan of parallel strokes. Only when that shape is really there does a word count as found.

What we look for in a line

With the transcription telling us which words are there and in what order, finding them becomes a matter of collecting clues. These are the ones we use most, with examples from the lines themselves.

The dictionary's own pictures
Horus and Seth was written in a very similar hand, so a dictionary photo of a word is the best reference there is. pA, for example, shows up as two bird-like flourishes side by side.
Determinatives as signposts
The sign at the end of a word often gives it away even when the rest is hard. In Two Brothers 4.2 we found Atp “load” by its man carrying a load, and in 4.3 bHA by the striking man, although neither word has a dictionary photo.
Repetition
A word that occurs twice confirms itself. The three ink “I” in Wenamun 2.12 and the three mtw.k in 2.33 anchored their lines. Across lines, mr in 1.13 was confirmed by the same shape in 1.22, and br and xbr by the same words in 2.15.
Red ink
Rubrics are words too. When the red has faded almost away we place the words by the proportions of the transcription. Better still, we space them like the same phrase in a line where it survives, as we did for Two Brothers 18.2 after 17.8.
Numbers
Upright strokes for units and an upside-down V for ten can be read without any reference, which is how we found the 11 at the end of Wenamun 1.11.
Small strokes and long tails
The n is often just a short low stroke under another sign, and k can be a long diagonal tail running under the word before it. The tail of an f can sweep under the next word. Recognising these is what lets a small word get its own box instead of disappearing into a neighbour.
Short writings
The scribe abbreviates. Sri “young” is sometimes only the bird with a very tall stroke, and “life, prosperity, health” can shrink to three thin strokes between two words.

The challenges that shaped the reading

Each of these came up in the first lines and changed how every line after them was read.

  • Hieratic is not laid out like the transcription. A sign printed stacked can be written on the baseline beside its neighbour (the n beside pr-aA in Two Brothers 10.8), and a pair printed side by side can be stacked. We follow the ink, not the printed layout.
  • Words that fuse. Small groups like f + r are written as one stroke cluster. When they cannot honestly be divided they share one box.
  • Words that look alike. HD “silver” and nbw “gold” sit side by side in Wenamun 2.11 and look much the same, so the border between them stays marked unsure.
  • The papyrus itself. Lines slope, the photo strip catches the lines above and below, and there are stains, cracks, holes and sheet joins. The Two Brothers photos are small too: some page 8 strips are 1,052 pixels wide for up to 41 words. Exact heights keep boxes on their own line, and damage is always marked.
  • Lines that do not end where words end. In Two Brothers 8.4 the last word, iwdn, starts again at the beginning of 8.5. The word is split across the line break.
  • Ink without a word, and marks that are not words. Left-over ink at the end of 19.8 led us back to Moldenke's book and to the words missing from the transcription. Printed page marks like “XVII” and stray strokes in the margin are left without a box.
  • Saying what we did not see. Every helper ended with a report on each line: which words were recognised, which were placed by order, and which boxes were not looked at again after the last change. When a helper could not view a line's pictures at all, it said so, and those lines were flagged as the least reliable.

05 Running it across both papyri

For Wenamun we split the 93 lines between helpers working side by side. For the Two Brothers, Katrine first approved one finished line (9.3) as the example of the quality we wanted, and every helper was shown it. The lines went out in batches of about ten, 22 helper runs in all. Tips from early batches were passed on to later ones: when the photo strip also catches the lines above and below, or the writing slopes, give every box an exact height so it stays on its own line.

06 The problems we hit, and what we did

None of the hard parts were solved in one go. Most of them were found by Katrine looking at the page and saying what was wrong. We changed the method so the same mistake could not come back.

Small strokes that only fill part of the line
In 9.3 Katrine saw that one n was only partly hit and the second n sat under its box, on top of the f below. Full-height boxes were too coarse for the small n and r strokes that the scribe tucks above and below other signs, so we added exact heights. In the end most Two Brothers boxes carry one.
Groups that cannot be split
The helpers had split psDt + nTrw (“Ennead of gods”). Katrine pointed out that the dictionary has it as one word, which is why the hieratic writes it as one group. Fused groups became one box shared by the words in it.
Words missing from the transcription
Two Brothers 19.8 had more ink than words. We checked Moldenke's book (p. 92) and added the missing Hri, sS and mr-m-ipt to the line before boxing them.
The wrong word
Some boxes were in the right place on the wrong word. One example: st “she” (11.10) and st-Hmt “woman” (12.1). Whenever a word was wrong in one place, we checked every place it occurs, not just the one that was spotted.
Boxes slightly off
nty in Wenamun 1.25, the last word of a line shifted to the left, Hri needing a push to the right. Small nudges like these were applied straight away and checked again on the photo before saving.
Labels running off the screen
The word label on the hieratic photo used to sit right above the word and got cut off at the edges. Now it is placed above or below the whole line, depending on where the reader has room, and laid out sideways when there is no room for all of it.
Showing honesty on the page
The switch on each line is called “AI annotation”. Unsure boxes are drawn dashed in orange, so nobody mistakes a guess for a reading. Hovering a word lights up its partner in the other picture.

07 Making the boxes useful

Once every word had a place, we could gather them. The Word Index collects every occurrence across all three manuscripts, with a picture of each one cut from the hieratic. The dictionary shows the same locations under “Also in” for every entry of the same word. The manuscript pages got a new Index tab in their search panel, built on the Two Brothers panel because it held the most information.

Grouping turned out to be the real work. We group by how the word is written, using the dictionary's spelling when the link is certain and Moldenke's names for words found only in the Two Brothers. Related forms are gathered into word families that open into their variants, and every search also shows the ten closest matches. The pictures add about 20 MB, so they are stored in 64 small files and only the ones being looked at are fetched.

08 Checking every word by hand

The AI pass gets the words close. A person has to make them right. Our first idea was a sheet of the five most common words to mark good or bad, and Katrine stopped it quickly: you cannot judge a box without seeing it on its line. So we built a review tool that shows one word at a time, on its own hieratic line, with the transcription beneath it and the word boxed in both.

In the tool Katrine moves the box, erases ink that belongs to a neighbour (or restores it), and fills in everything the dictionary holds on a word: hieroglyphs, transliteration, alternative spelling, English, Gardiner codes, gender and the line translation. The Gardiner codes follow the hieroglyphs as they are typed. A word can be marked good, unreadable or wrong. Nothing is carried over from one word to the next; an early version did that and produced eighteen wrong entries.

The loop we repeat for every batch

  1. Katrine reviews a word group

    Hr, n, iw, f, pA, r, i, m, sn, Dd, ir … and saves a progress file.

  2. We read it through

    What is new, what changed, and anything that looks odd: an empty hieroglyph field, a note, a spelling that does not match its meaning. Real questions go back to Katrine before anything is changed.

  3. The checked boxes go back into the pages

    Every word marked good replaces its AI box on the manuscript page and stops being shown as unsure.

  4. Everything is rebuilt

    Word index, pictures, the Excel database (laid out like the Horus and Seth database, with a separate sheet for words that are not usable) and a fresh copy of the review tool. Every file copied to Katrine's folder is checked against the original.

We have been through this loop about twenty times so far. The first check of Hr, the largest group, showed how close the AI boxes already were. Of 268 good Hr, 120 needed no change at all, 94 only a small nudge, and 54 a larger move.

10 What the review taught us about the text

Looking at every word this closely showed places where the word division itself was wrong. Fixing those changed the pages, not just the database.

r-iqr is one word
Katrine marked a lone r as wrong with the note “riqr”. We searched both papyri and found eleven places where r and iqr had been split, and joined them all.
sp-2 “twice” stands on its own
We took sp-2 out of ten combined words. On the photos we put the split in the wrong place and moved two signs into the wrong word without asking. Katrine caught it, and since then the rule is: when in doubt, ask first.
“Life, prosperity, health” is one word
anx wDA snb was split into three words in 43 places in the Two Brothers. It is now one word, as it is written.
Reading the signs themselves
A word in Two Brothers 17,06 was labelled Dd, but the transcription shows no cobra. Its signs (s43, d46, x1, a2) spell mdt “speaking”. In Wenamun, two prepositions written with n became n, r + Dd became one word rDd, and a supposed m that is not in the text at all went to “not usable”.
Keeping saved work in step
Every saved word is filed under its position in the line, so joining or splitting words shifts everything after it. The review tool now renumbers saved work by itself when it opens or loads a save file. We test each change three ways: Katrine's browser copy as it is now, an older copy, and her latest save file.
Talking about signs
Hieroglyphs typed into question boxes during our conversations came out as the wrong signs. We now name signs by Gardiner code when asking, and check every saved sign by its Unicode name.

Of the 2,032 words checked so far, 1,922 are good and cleaned, 104 are unreadable on the photo, and 6 turned out to be wrong. Every good word is now a clean hieratic picture with its full dictionary information, waiting in the new database to be added to the dictionary. How we work together

Katrine brings the eye and the Egyptology. She decides what a sign is, sees when a box is off, and checks every word. Claude builds the tools, runs the first pass with its helpers, keeps the bookkeeping straight and asks when something is unclear. Nearly every improvement in this report started as one short message from Katrine: “that's nty”, “one word, not 3”, “look at the glyphs”.

We are still pushing on and learning new methods all the time. Every mistake so far has shown us a new way to read the papyrus, and we expect the next ones to do the same.
